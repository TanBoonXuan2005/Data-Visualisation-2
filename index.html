Australian Heritage Sites

Australia's heritage spans natural landscapes, historic places and Indigenous culture. This visualisation examines datasets covering 126 National Heritage places and 21 Australian World Heritage properties. Explore where they are, what makes them significant, how people experience them and what threatens them.

Where is Australia's heritage?

Historic places cluster in the south-east: 54 of the 65 shown are in New South Wales, Victoria, Tasmania or the Australian Capital Territory (ACT), and 23 lie within 15 km of central Melbourne or Sydney. Natural places spread far more widely, with only 11 of 34 in those four jurisdictions.

Source: Australian Government, National Heritage List Spatial Database. Each point represents one listed place, rather than its complete geographic boundary. Six places in external territories, such as Mawson's Huts in Antarctica, and Macquarie Island are not shown, so 119 of the 126 places appear.

Measured against land area, the ACT stands out with about 212 listed places per 100,000 km², because four of its five places, including Old Parliament House and the Australian War Memorial, are national institutions in Canberra. Tasmania (19) and Victoria (13) follow; Queensland, Western Australia and the Northern Territory each have fewer than one.

Source: Australian Government, National Heritage List Spatial Database. Density is calculated using distinct listed places per 100,000 km² of state or territory land area.

Historic places dominate the south-east: 23 of Victoria's 30 places and four of the ACT's five are historic. In Western Australia, Queensland and the Northern Territory, at least three in four places are natural or Indigenous.

Places that span more than one state or territory are counted in each, so the columns add up to 130 rather than 126. What makes these places significant?

More than half of Australia's World Heritage properties (12 of 21) are listed for their natural values alone. Only five are purely cultural, and four are recognised for both.

Earth's history (criterion viii) is the most common reason for listing, applying to 12 of the 21 properties. No cultural criterion applies to more than four.

Criteria i to vi are cultural: human creativity, exchange of ideas, cultural traditions, buildings and landscapes, traditional land use, and links to beliefs or events. Criteria vii to x are natural: exceptional beauty, Earth's history, living ecosystems and biodiversity.

From 1981 to 2003, every Australian World Heritage property was natural or mixed. Five of the six inscribed since 2004 are cultural, including two Aboriginal cultural landscapes, Budj Bim (2019) and Murujuga (2025). Recognition can also change after inscription: Kakadu was extended in 1987 and 1992, the Tasmanian Wilderness in 1989 and the Gondwana Rainforests in 1994, and Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa was recognised for its cultural values in 1994.

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre. Diamonds show the significant boundary modifications recorded by UNESCO; minor boundary changes are not shown.

How do people experience Australia's World Heritage?

The Great Barrier Reef covers 34.9 million hectares, about three times the other 20 properties combined. The Sydney Opera House, at 5.8 hectares, is the smallest.

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre. Markers represent UNESCO reference coordinates and listed area. Heard and McDonald Islands and Macquarie Island are omitted from this mainland-focused view.

Parks Australia reports yearly visitor numbers for two of the labelled parks, Kakadu and Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa.

The parks followed different recovery paths. In 2020–21, Kakadu rose to 173,584 visitors while Uluṟu fell to 122,523. Since 2022–23, Uluṟu is up 11% and Kakadu down 8%. By 2024–25, Uluṟu had reached 267,363 visitors, still below its 2018–19 figure of 395,338, which was itself up 20% on the year before.

Source: Director of National Parks annual reports and DCCEEW FOI 80520. Data extracted and standardised by the author. Financial years run from July to June. Visitor-counting methods differ between the two parks, so comparisons focus on trends within each park.

What threatens Australia's World Heritage?

When a World Heritage property is under threat, UNESCO asks the government for a State of Conservation report. Since 1983 it has reviewed 14 of Australia's 21 properties in 88 reports. All 14 are natural or mixed properties; none of the five purely cultural properties has been reviewed.

Management problems, such as missing plans or funding, were raised for 10 properties, and mining or other extraction for seven, from Kakadu to the Blue Mountains. Climate change appears for only two, and 11 of its 13 reports concern the Great Barrier Reef.

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, State of Conservation reports for Australia, 1983 to 2026. Threats are the "factors affecting the property" UNESCO lists in each report, grouped into its categories with simplified names. The 1997 report on Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa recorded no specific threat.

In the 1990s and 2000s, more than half of all reports raised management problems. In the 2020s that fell to one report in 12, while transport and energy infrastructure (8 of 12 reports each), climate change and bushfires (5 of 12 each) took over.

Counted by how often UNESCO has listed them, climate threats come first (46 listings), but 44 of those are for the Great Barrier Reef. Mining, listed 36 times, is the most frequent single threat. Seventeen of the 35 types of threat were still being listed in reports from 2020 onwards.

The reef's latest survey brings some good news. Coral cover in the northern and central reef is above its long-term average, and the north is starting to recover from the 2024 mass bleaching. In the south, cover is 26.4%, 2.9 percentage points below its average.

Source: Australian Institute of Marine Science (AIMS), Annual Summary Report of Coral Reef Condition 2025/26; 121 reefs surveyed between August 2025 and June 2026. Hard coral cover is the share of the seabed covered by living hard coral. Because only a sample of reefs is surveyed, each figure is an estimate; the survey ranges shown on hover are AIMS's margins of error.
