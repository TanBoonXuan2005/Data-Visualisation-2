Australian Heritage Sites

Australia's heritage spans natural landscapes, historic places and Indigenous culture. This visualisation examines datasets covering 126 National Heritage places and 21 Australian World Heritage properties. Explore where they are, what makes them significant and how people experience them.

Where is Australia's heritage?

Of the 126 National Heritage places in the dataset, 119 are shown on this map. Historic places form the largest group, with 65 plotted sites, compared with 34 natural and 20 Indigenous places.

Source: Australian Government, National Heritage List Spatial Database. Each point represents one listed place, rather than its complete geographic boundary. Remote and offshore locations may fall outside the mainland-focused map view.

The ACT has the highest heritage density, with approximately 212 listed places per 100,000 km². Tasmania has around 19 and Victoria around 13. The ACT's small land area contributes to its exceptionally high density.

Source: Australian Government, National Heritage List Spatial Database. Density is calculated using distinct listed places per 100,000 km² of state or territory land area.

New South Wales and Victoria lead with 30 places each. Together they hold 59 of the 126 listed places, close to half.

Places that span more than one state or territory are counted in each, so the bars add up to 130 rather than 126.

What makes these places significant?

More than half of Australia's World Heritage properties (12 of 21) are listed for their natural values alone. Only five are purely cultural, and four are recognised for both.

Earth's history (criterion viii) is the most common reason for listing, applying to 12 of the 21 properties. No cultural criterion applies to more than four.

Criteria i to vi are cultural: human creativity, exchange of ideas, cultural traditions, buildings and landscapes, traditional land use, and links to beliefs or events. Criteria vii to x are natural: exceptional beauty, Earth's history, living ecosystems and biodiversity.

From 1981 to 2003, every Australian World Heritage property was natural or mixed. Five of the six inscribed since 2004 are cultural, including two Aboriginal cultural landscapes, Budj Bim (2019) and Murujuga (2025). Recognition can also change after inscription: Kakadu was extended in 1987 and 1992, and Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa was recognised for its cultural values in 1994.

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre. Additional dates reflect the selected UNESCO dataset fields and do not represent a complete history of all decisions.

How do people experience Australia's World Heritage?

The Great Barrier Reef covers 34.9 million hectares, about three times the other 20 properties combined. The Sydney Opera House, at 5.8 hectares, is the smallest.

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre. Markers represent UNESCO reference coordinates and listed area. Heard and McDonald Islands and Macquarie Island are omitted from this mainland-focused view.

Among these World Heritage properties, Kakadu and Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa attract visitors from across the world. Their annual visitor numbers reveal different recovery patterns.

The parks followed different recovery paths. In 2020–21, Kakadu received 173,584 visitors while Uluṟu received 122,523. From 2022–23 to 2024–25, Uluṟu's visitation increased by 10.6%, while Kakadu's declined by 8.0%. Uluṟu's 2018–19 total of 395,338 was itself 20% higher than the previous year.

Source: Director of National Parks annual reports and DCCEEW FOI 80520. Data extracted and standardised by the author. Financial years run from July to June. Visitor-counting methods differ between the two parks, so comparisons focus on trends within each park.
